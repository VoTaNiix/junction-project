How the pitch follows Junction's advice

Junction lists five dos and five don'ts. Here is where each one shows up in our pitch.

Do

Start with a story

After a 5-second team intro, we open with Sam, a real persona from the demo. The same Sam closes the pitch.

Do

Be user-centric

The problem is told from both sides of the table, and the demo shows what the solver, the host and the event team each see.

Do

Show a prototype

A 50-second live demo of working code, following one conversation from the solver to the host to the event team. Almost half the pitch.

Do

Introduce the team

Names on the first and last slides, said in one line at the start. There's no time for a team slide, so let the demo show what you can do.

Do

Practice

Use the timer above. Rehearse at least three times, once in front of someone outside the team.

Don't

Overload on jargon

Say "anonymous until both agree", not "pseudonymity". Say "skills list" before you say "ESCO". No stack talk unless asked.

Don't

Oversell

40% is a pilot target, not a result. The data is made up. Say "designed with the EU AI Act in mind", never "compliant".

Don't

Run long

Six slides, two minutes, one idea per slide. Everything else moved to backup slides for questions. Stop talking at the closing line.

Don't

Dodge questions

The Q&A section below has short answers to the likely questions, including the uncomfortable ones.

Don't

Ignore feedback

Two feedback rounds already shaped the product (fewer sections, a simpler footer). Mention it if asked how you iterated.

Live demo script

50 seconds, from 0:40 to 1:30. One conversation seen from three sides. We ran these exact clicks on the final version after a reset, and every step worked. Start with the editor already open and question 2 filled in (see the checklist).

  1. Talent · 0:40–0:50

    • In the message at the bottom, click See it as Lumivirta Energy.

    "No CV, no photo. Three short answers. Now let's switch sides."

  2. Employer · 0:50–1:05
    • On the Review dashboard, click EU-Hosted Machine Translation (Simulated) under the Finnish answer from Solver #ESP-2093.

    "No names, no scores, oldest first. The translation sits next to the Finnish, never in its place."

  3. Employer · 1:05–1:15
    • Open Conversations and click Accept.

    "Only now does Sam's name appear."

  4. Event team · 1:15–1:30
    • Click Event team in the header.
    • Click Approve on Lumivirta Energy and Solver #ESP-4821. It is the third card, so don't click the first one by habit.
    • Open Schedule and export and point at the three download buttons. Don't click them.

    "A person confirms every meeting, and it goes straight to Brella, Tavata or your calendar."

If the demo breaks

Don't debug on stage. Say "Let me show you what you would have seen" and jump to slide 7, the backup with three screenshots, right after the closing slide. Walk through it in 20 seconds with the same lines, then go back to slide 5, "Fair by design".

If something only looks wrong (a step already done, a different card on top), the data was not reset. Keep talking over it, or click Reset demo data once and carry on. Reset takes two clicks.

Q&A prep

Short answers first, details only if they ask a follow-up. If you don't know, say so and say what you would do to find out.

Can't people just use AI to write their answers too?

They can, but it helps less. The answers are about one specific problem, and the third question is what you would ask the host. Generic polish doesn't help there. More importantly, the answer is not the decision: it only gets you into a real conversation, and you can't outsource that.

How is this different from Brella or LinkedIn?

Brella and Tavata arrange the meetings; we decide who should meet and why, then export to them. LinkedIn is built around profiles and CVs, which is exactly what we leave out until both sides agree to meet.

Why don't you use AI to match people? Wouldn't that scale better?

AI in hiring counts as high-risk under the EU AI Act, and automated scoring tends to reward polish and pedigree. We chose to keep people deciding. The five-request limit per person keeps the volume readable, and answers are short.

What stops a company from getting flooded?

Each solver can send at most five conversation requests, and the event team approves every meeting. Answers are three short questions, and bullet points are fine, so reading them is quick.

Who pays for this?

Be honest that it's not validated. The natural buyer is the event organizer, here the City of Espoo and Business Espoo, as part of running an event. A pilot would tell us whether companies would also pay per challenge. Don't name a price you haven't tested.

What about privacy and GDPR?

Consent is for one purpose, arranging conversations at this event. Hosts see a solver ID, not a name. Data is deleted 30 days after the event, or straight away when someone withdraws consent. We never ask for age, gender, nationality, photo or school.

Does it work for non-tech companies?

Yes. The demo already includes public-sector services, construction and manufacturing. Any company with a real problem can describe it, and the 10-minute template walks them through it.

What did you actually build, and what is mocked?

The app is real: a Scala server holds the state and every rule, and the frontend is plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript in three languages. The data is made up, and the machine translation is simulated and labelled that way in the app.

How would you know it works?

One number: the share of conversations where both sides choose to keep talking afterwards. Our pilot target is 40%. After the event, each side privately picks "Let's continue" or "Not now", and contact details are shared only when both say continue.

What would you do next with more time?

Run it at one real Espoo event with a handful of hosts, connect the live ESCO skills list, add sign-in, and test accessibility with real users.

Rehearsal plan

Round 1: timing

Run it with the timer. Two minutes is tight: cut words wherever you go over, never the demo or the closing line. Aim for 1:50 to leave a buffer.

Round 2: an outsider

Pitch to someone outside the team. Ask them to retell the idea in one sentence. If they can't, fix the slide where they got lost.

Round 3: the real thing

Full run with the projector or a big screen, the clicker, and a reset before you start. Then two minutes of mock questions.
